Lunar Trailblazer Science

There’s something funny about the Moon being full of places called seas.

Early astronomers looked up and saw those dark patches and thought they were oceans, so they called them maria—Latin for seas. They were wrong. The Moon is less ocean and more desert: vast basalt plains, mountains, craters, dust, and an almost complete lack of anything that would make a human feel particularly welcome.

Except there’s water there.

Not rivers or oceans. Ice. Hydroxyl. Water bound to minerals. Water hiding in permanently shadowed craters.

So, in a strange way, they weren’t completely wrong.

I was brought into Lunar Trailblazer by the mission’s principal investigator to help develop its visual identity and communications. It was a particularly good fit: I come from a geology background, and I’ve always been interested in the place where scientific information becomes something people can actually see and understand.

Lunar Trailblazer is a small spacecraft with a very specific job: find water on the Moon.

Simple enough.

Except “find water on the Moon” turns out to contain multitudes.

There’s water ice. Hydroxyl. Chemically bound water. Spectral signatures. Mineralogy. Orbital mechanics. Infrared spectroscopy. Permanently shadowed regions. Instruments with names that sound like minor characters in a hard-science-fiction novel.

And then there’s the small matter of communicating all of it in a single visual.

That was my problem. Per aspera ad luna, or: through challenges, to the Moon.

GETTING LOST IN THE SCIENCE

Before I could figure out what the graphic should look like, I had to figure out what the hell I was looking at.

I consumed a glut of research papers, technical documents, mission resources, imagery, and scientific literature—both internally with the Trailblazer team and externally.

I dug through The New York Times science section. National Geographic infographics. Books detailing lunar water discoveries dating back to the 1960s. Galileo Galilei’s drawings of the Moon.

I looked at other lunar missions, spacecraft representation, scientific data visualization, geology diagrams, decades of space exploration.

And then the research started doing what research does when you aren't careful: it became interesting.

The research had its own gravity.

One question pulled me toward another. Water led to spectroscopy. Spectroscopy led to mineralogy. Mineralogy led to geology. Geology led to craters, craters led to the lunar poles, and the lunar poles led me to some of the coolest photographs of the Moon I’d ever seen.

Things started to accumulate mass.

At some point, I wasn't just researching the mission anymore. I was orbiting it.

SCIENCE LITERACY, OR: PLEASE DON'T MAKE ME MEMORIZE A TEXTBOOK

I care about science literacy. Not necessarily the “here are five fun facts about Saturn!” variety. I’m more interested in the moment when something intimidating suddenly becomes legible.

When the diagram you’ve been staring at finally clicks. When you realize that a weird squiggly line isn’t just a weird squiggly line—it’s telling you something about what a material is made of.

I didn’t want to make the science simpler.

I wanted to make it legible.

There’s a difference.

Simplification can remove information. Legibility gives you a way into it.

ACCURACY IS A DESIGN PROBLEM

Scientists don’t necessarily care if your graphic is attractive.

They care if it’s right.

My job was to care about both.

There’s a famous Neil deGrasse Tyson story about Titanic: he noticed that the night sky shown in the film wasn’t actually the sky passengers would have seen that night. A tiny detail, but the kind of thing an expert notices immediately.

That became a useful reminder for this project: somebody, somewhere, is going to notice the sky.

When you’re working with people who know planetary science for a living, the feedback can get wonderfully specific.

“The radiator on the HVM3 instrument would malfunction if it were facing the Sun.”

Or:

“The rim around…”

And suddenly you’re three levels deeper into spacecraft engineering than you planned to be that afternoon.

Not maliciously.
Precisely.

A line might imply the wrong relationship. An orbit might suggest something physically impossible. A diagram might contain all the right information while still communicating the wrong idea.

That tension was useful.

I was trying to push the visual language toward something more scientific and rigorous without sacrificing accessibility—or making something that only another planetary scientist could understand.

The scientists forced me to defend the design. The design forced me to think harder about how someone outside the field would understand it.

That back-and-forth made the work better.

FINDING THE STORY

So what does Trailblazer actually do?

It follows a trail around the Moon, looks through a defined footprint, and collects spectral fingerprints that help distinguish water, ice, hydroxyl, minerals, and other materials on the lunar surface.

That gave me a simple framework:

FINGERPRINT
What it finds.
FOOTPRINT
Where it looks.
TRAIL
How it gets there.
THE EYE
What is doing the looking.

The system became less about illustrating individual scientific concepts and more about finding the relationships between them.

Follow the trail. Find the footprint. Read the fingerprint. Look for water.

TURNING SCIENCE INTO A VISUAL SYSTEM

So what does invisible information look like?

The logo became a place to start.

The central water droplet represents the mission’s central quarry: lunar water. The hexagonal geometry references crystalline structures, ice, minerals, and the geological language of the Moon. The expanding contour lines suggest both lunar topography and spectral fingerprints. The square field references the spacecraft’s imaging footprint.

From there, the visual language could expand naturally: technical linework, contours, measurement marks, spectral graphs, orbital paths, geological forms, data.

The science wasn’t decoration. It was the visual language.

The palette draws from the Moon itself: cool mineral blues for water, ice, and shadow; warm oranges and rusts for sunlight, heat, and iron-rich terrain; near-black for space and permanently shadowed regions.

Cold and heat.
Light and shadow.
Water and rock.

THE ART OF GETTING IT WRONG

None of this arrived fully formed.

There were thumbnail sketches. Rough diagrams. Bad ideas. Better ideas. Versions that looked promising until you actually tried to explain them.

Some looked beautiful but explained nothing.

Others explained everything and looked like a hostage situation involving PowerPoint.

The questions became:

Is it useful?
Is it accurate?
Does it create curiosity?
Or does it just look cool?

The last one is occasionally a perfectly valid reason.

It just can’t be the only one.

MAKING COMPLEXITY FEEL SIMPLE

The final compositions needed to hold spacecraft motion, instruments, lunar terrain, water signatures, geology, data, and the relationship between the spacecraft and the surface below it.

The trick wasn’t to remove the complexity.

It was to give the complexity a hierarchy.

A good diagram doesn’t make a complicated subject less complicated. It gives you somewhere to start.

You don’t need to understand spectroscopy before you look at a spectral graph. You don’t need to understand orbital mechanics before you follow a trajectory. You don’t need a geology degree before a crater becomes interesting.

Design can give complexity a door.

You just have to make someone want to open it.

THE RESULT

Lunar Trailblazer became less about making a collection of attractive graphics and more about building a visual language for a mission that is, at its core, looking for things we can’t simply see.

The project sits somewhere between art and science, information and emotion, precision and play.

Not the idea that planetary science is easy. It isn’t.

Not the idea that design can flatten billions of years of lunar history into one clever graphic. It can’t.

But it can give people a way in.

And sometimes that’s enough to start pulling them in.
